\begin{abstract}
\noindent This note answers three questions about the trading work in the
repository: (i) \emph{are the trading results OK?}, (ii) \emph{which strategies
were tried, on which pairs?}, and (iii) \emph{does the final paper}
(\code{latex/optimal\_long\_short.tex}) \emph{include the most relevant use
case(s)?} The short answers: the results are internally coherent and deliver a
clean, honest \emph{negative} result (the naive leveraged carry book is a bad
trade). The Sharpe convention has now been fixed (Section~\ref{sec:sharpefix}):
the benchmark previously scored every strategy against a hardcoded flat $5\%$
and mixed an arithmetic Sharpe numerator with a geometric Sortino numerator.
After correcting both --- risk-free set to the realised on-chain USDC supply
rate, and Sharpe/Sortino/Omega placed on one consistent excess series --- the
Sharpe ratios stay negative, but now for a \emph{genuine economic reason}: the
mean USDC supply rate over the window was $4.80\%$, and \emph{no} strategy beat
simply supplying USDC. Eight distinct strategies were run across a 37-pair cross-section, with
almost all backtesting on \code{USDC}(supply)/\code{WETH}(borrow) and the
analytics on \code{WBTC}/\code{WETH}. The final paper contains the diagnostic
use cases (sizing, carry-inert cross-section, regime switching, depeg, recovery)
but \textbf{omits the single most actionable use case --- the Taleb barbell ---}
which the historical benchmark identifies as the best strategy on a tail-risk
basis. The paper's negative result is present; its constructive answer is not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Are the trading results OK?}

\paragraph{Verdict.} \emph{Directionally yes, with one methodological caveat that
must be resolved before publication.} The results are consistent with the
paper's thesis and with the pre-registered cross-section: the leveraged
long--short / carry book on Aave is structurally short two convexities (the
liquidation barrier and the utilisation kink) and collects negative net carry on
every ETH/BTC-collateral pair, so it should --- and does --- lose money once
liquidations are counted. That is a genuine, valuable finding, not a bug.

\paragraph{What is solid.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The negative result reproduces out of sample.} In the
  walk-forward file (\code{optimal\_long\_short/walk\_forward\_oos\_results.csv},
  19 non-overlapping 45-day entries, 2023-11 $\to$ 2026-02) the naive carry leg
  is liquidated in 5/19 windows and posts large negative PnL in several more; the
  barbell and pure-supply legs stay small and bounded.
  \item \textbf{The cross-section is unambiguous.} In
  \code{results/pair\_cross\_section.csv}, \emph{every} ETH- and BTC-collateral
  pair has negative net carry (roughly $-1.8\%$ to $-4.5\%$/yr); only stable/stable
  legs (e.g.\ \code{USDC/USDe}) show marginally positive carry. This is the
  structural premise of the whole framework and it holds across all 37 pairs.
  \item \textbf{The Monte-Carlo carry study is coherent.}
  \code{results/aave\_carry\_trade\_metrics.csv} (CIR vs.\ CIR+Jump, 10\,000 paths)
  gives $\sim$6.7--6.9\% liquidation probability with $\sim$91--96\% of surviving
  paths profitable --- a sensible ``small edge, fat left tail'' shape.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{The Sharpe convention (now fixed --- see Section~\ref{sec:sharpefix}).}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Every strategy has a negative Sharpe ratio} in the 5-strategy
  historical benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}), \emph{including} strategies
  with positive cumulative return (the barbell: $+15.1\%$ cumulative, $+4.3\%$
  annualised). The original code had two defects: it benchmarked against a flat
  hardcoded $5\%$ risk-free, and it computed the Sharpe numerator arithmetically
  while computing the Sortino numerator geometrically. Both are fixed. The
  surprise: the correction \emph{does not} flip the signs --- the realised mean
  USDC supply rate was $4.80\%$, so the flat $5\%$ was nearly right, and the
  negative Sharpes are economically real: \textbf{no leveraged strategy beat the
  $\sim$$4.8\%$ risk-free of simply supplying USDC}. This is a sharper finding
  than the original, not a bug artifact. (Sortino moved materially, e.g.\ barbell
  $-0.65 \to -0.08$, because the inconsistent numerator is gone.)
  \item \textbf{The carry blow-up is a total wipeout} ($-100\%$ cumulative,
  Sharpe $-3.31$, max drawdown $-100\%$). That is a knockout artifact (equity
  hits zero once, path ends), which is \emph{correct} as a statement that the
  trade can be ruined, but it should be reported as ``ruin probability / time to
  ruin,'' not as a return series, since a $-100\%$ return dominates every summary
  statistic it enters.
  \item \textbf{Sign conventions on the risk columns are inconsistent}
  (e.g.\ \code{VaR 95\%} is reported as $-5.44$ for the barbell, i.e.\ a gain,
  but as $+5886$ for carry). Standardise before publishing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{The Sharpe convention fix}\label{sec:sharpefix}
The metric helper (\code{compute\_metrics} in \code{strategy\_benchmark.ipynb})
was corrected in two ways, and the notebook re-executed:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Risk-free = on-chain USDC supply rate.} The book is
  USDC-denominated, so the opportunity cost is what idle capital earns supplying
  USDC on Aave, not a flat exogenous $5\%$. A per-day \code{RF\_DAILY} series is
  built from the realised supply APR (geometric annual$\to$daily) and used as the
  excess-return base, aligned to each strategy's dates.
  \item \textbf{Consistent numerators.} Sharpe, Sortino and Omega now use the
  \emph{same} per-period excess series $r-r_f$ and the same arithmetic
  $\sqrt{365}$ annualisation. Previously Sharpe used an arithmetic excess mean
  while Sortino used a geometric $(\text{CAGR}-r_f)$ numerator --- an internal
  inconsistency that made the two ratios non-comparable.
\end{enumerate}
Net effect: Sharpe barely moves (the flat $5\%$ was coincidentally close to the
$4.80\%$ realised supply rate), Sortino moves materially, and the qualitative
conclusion is unchanged and now defensible.

\noindent\textbf{Bottom line.} The economics are right and the negative result
is real and well-supported. With the Sharpe convention now fixed, the honest
reading is: over Jan 2023--Jun 2026 \emph{no} leveraged strategy out-earned the
$\sim$$4.8\%$ risk-free of just supplying USDC on a risk-adjusted basis; the
barbell and momentum rotation are the only two that preserve capital (positive
Calmar, bounded drawdown) and are the sensible choices for a tail-averse
allocator. The one remaining cosmetic item is the VaR sign convention (negative
= gain for the near-riskless strategies), which is confusing but not a bug.

\paragraph{The most relevant use case is missing.} The paper's central thesis is
a \emph{negative} one: the leveraged long--short book is structurally a bad
trade. The \textbf{barbell} (\code{barbell.py}) is the direct constructive
response --- it longs exactly the two convexities the levered book is short (the
barrier via a keeper leg, the utilisation kink via a supplier leg) --- and the
historical benchmark ranks it \emph{first} on Calmar (1.66) and tail metrics,
with a bounded $-2.6\%$ max drawdown versus the carry trade's $-100\%$. It is
the single most actionable output of the whole project, and it appears \emph{only}
in code and an executed notebook, never in the paper. This mirrors the standing
critique in \code{CRITICAL\_REPORT.md} \S3.4: the paper diagnoses that
mean--variance gives no interior optimum but never evaluates the growth-optimal /
convexity-harvesting alternative that would.

\paragraph{Recommendation.} Add one section to
\code{optimal\_long\_short.tex} --- ``From the negative result to a tradable
barbell'' --- that (i) states the two short-convexity exposures formally, (ii)
prices the supplier and keeper legs with the existing first-passage transform,
and (iii) reports the tail-risk-dominant backtest, \emph{after} fixing the
Sharpe/VaR convention bug flagged in Section~1. Without it, the paper delivers
the diagnosis but withholds the cure that the codebase already computes.
